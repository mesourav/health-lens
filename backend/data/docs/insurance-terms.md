# Insurance Terms Explained

## What is an ex gratia payment?
An ex gratia payment (from Latin, "out of kindness") is money the insurer chooses to pay on a
claim that the policy does not strictly have to cover. It is a goodwill gesture, not a right, and
it does not mean similar claims will be paid in the future. Ex gratia payments are decided case by
case, usually after a review or an appeal.

## What is a waiting period?
A waiting period is the time after your policy starts during which certain treatments are not yet
covered. For example, planned treatments for some conditions may only be covered after the waiting
period ends. Emergency care is covered from day one. Your policy document lists which waiting
periods apply to you.

## What is a pre-existing condition?
A pre-existing condition is a health condition you had before your policy started, such as
diabetes, asthma or high blood pressure. Your plan covers care for pre-existing conditions. You will
not be refused coverage or charged more because of one.

## What is an exclusion?
An exclusion is something the policy does not cover at all. Common exclusions are cosmetic
procedures that are not medically necessary and experimental treatments. Exclusions are listed in
your policy document.

## What does medically necessary mean?
A service is medically necessary when a doctor decides it is needed to diagnose or treat an
illness, injury or condition, and it meets accepted medical standards. Care that is only for comfort
or appearance is usually not medically necessary and may not be covered.

## What is a grace period?
A grace period is the extra time you get to pay your premium after its due date without losing
coverage. If the premium is still unpaid when the grace period ends, the policy can lapse. Turning
on autopay avoids missed payments.

## What does it mean if my policy lapses?
A policy lapses when premiums are not paid by the end of the grace period, and coverage stops. A
lapsed policy can sometimes be reinstated by paying the overdue premium. Contact a member advocate
as soon as possible if your policy has lapsed.

## What is a cashless claim?
A cashless claim means the insurer pays an in-network hospital or doctor directly, so you only pay
your share, such as a copay or coinsurance, at the time of care. You do not pay the full bill and
wait to be paid back.

## What is a reimbursement claim?
A reimbursement claim means you pay the full bill yourself first and then send the bills and
receipts to the insurer to be paid back for the covered amount. This usually happens with
out-of-network care.

## What is a rider or add-on?
A rider (also called an add-on) is extra coverage you can buy on top of your main plan, such as
dental or vision cover. It has its own price and its own rules.

## What is a sub-limit?
A sub-limit is a cap on how much the plan pays for one particular type of care, even if your
overall coverage is higher. For example, a plan can limit the number of physical therapy visits it
pays for each year.

## What is a TPA (third-party administrator)?
A third-party administrator, or TPA, is a company that handles claims and paperwork on behalf of
the insurer. If a TPA contacts you about a claim, they are working for your insurance plan.

## What is a free look period?
A free look period is a short time after you buy a new policy when you can review it and cancel
it for a refund of the premium if it is not right for you. The exact length of the free look
period is printed in your welcome letter.

## What is balance billing?
Balance billing is when an out-of-network provider bills you for the difference between their full
charge and what the plan paid. In-network providers are not allowed to balance bill you.

## What is coordination of benefits?
Coordination of benefits is how two health plans share the cost when you are covered by both, for
example your own plan and a spouse's plan. One plan pays first, and the second may pay part of
what is left, so the total paid never exceeds the bill.
